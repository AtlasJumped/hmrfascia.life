The body as a tensegrity

Researchers in anatomy, cell biology, and fascia science have argued for decades that the human body is built as a tensegrity: rigid bones that do not stack like bricks, but float in a continuous, pre-tensioned web of fascia and connective tissue.

Tensegrities obey physics we understand very well. So we can take measurements of real, imperfect human bodies and ask a precise question: what is mechanically a better shape than this? Asked again and again, that question defines the optimized tensegrity, where tension is shared evenly, every layer glides, and nothing strains.

The step from the researchers' work to an optimized human tensegrity is ours.

Established science

Stephen Levin, 1980

The skeleton, rather than being a frame of support, has to be considered as compression components suspended within a continuous tension network.

Source: Continuous Tension, Discontinuous Compression

Established science

Randel Swanson, 2013

Research over 25 years has demonstrated that the architectural principles of tensegrity can be applied to biological organisms, termed biotensegrity.

Source: J Am Osteopath Assoc

Established science

Donald Ingber, 2023

Hierarchical tensegrities stabilize form at all size scales, from molecules and cells to tissues, organs, and whole organisms. Living cells behave as prestressed tensegrities.

Source: Biochemical Journal

How every tensegrity behaves

The cables are tight before any load arrives, so the structure holds itself. A force does not stay where it lands, because the whole web carries it. Press one corner and every part gives slightly, then returns. A few slender parts make a structure that is firm for its weight.

Fascia first

In HMR, connective tissue (often called fascia) is not packing material. It is a continuous, living web that holds the body's shape under tension, carries force from one region to another, lets layers slide over each other, and is densely supplied with nerves.

When fascia is healthy it is hydrated, mobile, and evenly tensioned. When it becomes denser or stuck in one place, the tension of the whole web shifts: posture changes, movement narrows, and the vessels, nerves, and organs that run through the tissue can be squeezed or strained.

Established science

Layers built to glide

Hyaluronan between the sheets of deep fascia facilitates the free sliding of two adjacent fibrous layers (Stecco and others, 2011).

Established science

What happens when gliding is lost

Thoracolumbar fascia shear strain was about 20% lower in 121 people with chronic low back pain: 56.4% versus 70.2% (Langevin and others, 2011).

Established science

Ordinary stretching

Measured stiffness changes return to baseline within 10 to 20 minutes (Ryan and others, 2008).

Hypothesis

Receptive fascia

In HMR's experience, once fascia has been attended to directly, from the outside in and the inside out, it becomes highly receptive to change, and the changes hold and build on each other day after day. This is an HMR hypothesis.

The ideal body as a reference

Today, health is defined by what goes wrong. In HMR, it is defined by what fully right looks like, and every condition becomes a measurable distance from it. We are building, from real data, a model of an ideal adult body in male and female form, in layers: skeleton, fascia, everything inside, mechanics, movement, and experience.

Hypothesis

Disease as deviation

Once the ideal exists, it can be made imperfect on purpose. Each imperfection is a precise, measurable departure, such as two layers of tissue that no longer slide. The model asks what follows: where the body's shape shifts, and what the person would feel.

Hypothesis

Reverse-engineering disease

Many conditions change the body's shape in ways that can be photographed and measured. Comparing those shapes against the ideal asks what sequence of imperfections leads from a healthy body to this one.

Hypothesis

The densification hypothesis

Making fascia denser in a chosen place, while leaving the rest ideal, will by tension alone change the whole body's shape, leave other regions slacker, and strain what runs through the tissue. The prediction is that the effects will match the measurable signs of real diseases.

It fails wherever densification produces only local effects, or where the shapes do not match people who live with the disease. It is a hypothesis to be tested by simulation. It is not a medical claim, a diagnosis, or a treatment.

Even if you disagree about reality

A reference model of the healthy body, and disease measured as deviation from it, can be built, tested, and used on purely scientific terms. That is the part we put forward first.

Care: outside in, and inside out

Care starts with the fascia web, and it can be reached from two directions. Most care moves home, and the rest goes fascia-first.

HMR's view

From the surface

Pressure, scraping, stretching, vibration, heat, movement, and hydration applied from outside the body, to free tissue.

HMR's view

From within

The mind putting the body into positions and keeping it there:

  • Long holds, held for minutes, not seconds.
  • Breath, internal locks, sound, posture, attention, hydration, and nutrition: the internal methods that the world's body practices have used.
  • Having fun: running around, playing, sports.
  • Exercise, done while you think of yourself as a tensegrity.

HMR's view

Everyone, every day

Every person knows how to care for their own fascia, as ordinarily as brushing their teeth, and does it daily from childhood.

Hypothesis

A prediction to test

HMR predicts that if everyone practiced self-sufficient fascia care, about 90% of today's doctor visits would no longer be needed. It is a prediction, stated so that it can be tested, not a finding.

Fixing exercise: tensegrity first

Most exercise programs assume the body that is training is already sound. In HMR, few adult bodies are, and an imperfect tensegrity carries force unevenly. Where fascia has become denser, shorter, or stuck, those lines carry far more than their share, and the lines around them go slack. Training it harder makes the imperfections stronger.

That is HMR's view, and the injury figures below are published research.

HMR's view

HMR's order

1. Define the optimized tensegrity, with a physics model of the ideal body.

2. Mature the web toward it: hydration, long holds, hands-on work, and inside-out practices.

3. Then train. Once force is shared evenly, strength, speed, and endurance build on a sound structure instead of on its faults.

HMR's view

Start with children

The highest priority is children, before and during puberty, when the web is still being built and is easiest to shape. HMR's plan is to teach everyone what an optimized tensegrity is and make fascia care as ordinary as brushing teeth.

What we are careful about

Every claim is marked as established evidence or as hypothesis. The ideal body is a model to be tested, not a fact. We collect the studies that challenge the model as carefully as the ones that support it, and say plainly where it does not yet hold.
